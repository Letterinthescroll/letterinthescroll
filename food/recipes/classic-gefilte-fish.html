Classic Gefilte Fish with Chrain

Handmade poached fish patties from a blend of carp, whitefish, and pike, served cold with the traditional spicy beet horseradish known as chrain. A cornerstone of Ashkenazi holiday tables.

Prep Time45 min
Cook Time1 hr 15 min
Total Time2 hrs + chilling
Servings12 patties
DifficultyAdvanced
Passover Rosh Hashanah Shabbat

Ingredients

Adjust servings:
  • 3 lbs carp, filleted and ground
  • 1.5 lbs whitefish, filleted and ground
  • 1.5 lbs pike, filleted and ground
  • 6 medium onions, finely minced or grated
  • 6 large eggs
  • 3 tbsp granulated sugar
  • 1 cup matzah meal
  • 3/4 cup ice water
  • 2 tsp salt
  • 1 tsp white pepper
  • For the poaching stock:
  • Fish heads and bones (reserved from filleting)
  • 6 large carrots, peeled and left whole
  • 3 onions, quartered
  • 4 celery stalks with leaves
  • 1 tbsp salt
  • 1 tsp black peppercorns
  • 10 cups cold water
  • For chrain:
  • 1 large fresh horseradish root, peeled and grated
  • 2 medium cooked beets, grated
  • 2 tbsp white vinegar
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • Salt to taste

Directions

  1. Start by making the poaching stock, as it needs to be simmering before you add the fish. Place the fish heads, bones, and trimmings in a very large, wide stockpot or kettle. Add the quartered onions, whole carrots, celery stalks, salt, and peppercorns. Pour in 10 cups of cold water. Bring to a boil over high heat, then immediately reduce to a gentle simmer. Skim off any gray foam that rises to the surface during the first 10 minutes. Let the stock simmer while you prepare the fish mixture — it should cook for at least 20 minutes before you add the patties.
  2. In a very large mixing bowl, combine all three types of ground fish with the finely minced onions, eggs, sugar, matzah meal, ice water, salt, and white pepper. Mix the mixture thoroughly with your hands or a wooden spoon for 3 to 4 minutes until it becomes sticky, smooth, and holds together when you squeeze a handful. The ice water is important — it keeps the mixture cold during handling, which results in a lighter, more delicate texture in the finished gefilte fish.
  3. Wet your hands thoroughly with cold water. Take a generous handful of the fish mixture (about 1/3 cup) and shape it into a fat oval patty, roughly 3 inches long and 2 inches wide. The patties should be plump and well-formed — not too thin, or they will fall apart during poaching. Place each formed patty on a wet plate as you work. You should get approximately 12 patties.
  4. Carefully slide the fish patties into the simmering stock one at a time, spacing them apart. Try to arrange them in a single layer if possible. The stock should barely cover the tops of the patties — add a splash of water if needed. Return the liquid to a very gentle simmer (never a full boil, which would cause the patties to break apart).
  5. Cover the pot partially with a lid and let the gefilte fish poach at a gentle simmer for 25 to 30 minutes. The patties are done when they feel firm to the touch and have turned uniformly opaque. Do not stir or flip them during cooking.
  6. Turn off the heat and let the gefilte fish cool completely in the pot, submerged in the stock. This is essential — as the stock cools, it will form a natural jelly that is a traditional hallmark of homemade gefilte fish. Once cool, carefully transfer the patties and carrot slices to a storage container. Strain the stock through a fine-mesh sieve and pour it over the fish. Refrigerate overnight or for at least 6 hours.
  7. To make the chrain, combine the freshly grated horseradish with the grated cooked beets, white vinegar, sugar, and a pinch of salt. Mix well and taste — it should be bracingly sharp with a sweet beet undertone. Adjust vinegar and sugar to your preference. Store in a sealed jar in the refrigerator.
  8. To serve, remove the gefilte fish from the jellied stock and arrange on a platter. Place a round of poached carrot on top of each patty. Serve cold with a generous spoonful of chrain on the side.
Chef's Tip

The fish heads and bones in the poaching liquid are what create the natural jelly — do not skip them. Ask your fishmonger to save them when filleting. For the lightest texture, some cooks add a tablespoon of seltzer water to the fish mixture. The gefilte fish tastes best made a full day ahead.
