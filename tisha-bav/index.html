About the Day

Introduction

What is Tisha B'Av?

מהו תשעה באב

Hebrew Date: 9th of Av

Upcoming Date (2026): Begins evening of Wednesday, July 22, 2026

Meaning: "The Ninth of Av"

Duration: 25 hours — from sundown to nightfall the next day

Tisha B'Av, which translates to the 9th day of the Hebrew month of Av (typically falling in July or August), is the darkest and most mournful day in the Jewish year. It is a 25-hour period of intense fasting and collective mourning that commemorates a series of catastrophic tragedies that have befallen the Jewish people throughout history, most notably the destruction of both the First and Second Holy Temples in Jerusalem.

The Unbroken Yearning for Home

To understand Tisha B'Av is to understand the Jewish historical experience of exile, loss, and the unbroken, multi-millennial yearning to return home. Unlike the joyous festivals of the Jewish calendar, Tisha B'Av strips away every comfort so that the nation can sit — quite literally — with its grief.

Origins

Biblical Roots

The Night of Unwarranted Weeping

לֵיל בְּכִיָּה לְדוֹרוֹת

The Twelve Spies

The curse of the 9th of Av did not actually begin with the destruction of the Temples; it began in the desert. As recorded in the Book of Numbers (Parshat Sh'lach), Moses sent twelve spies to scout the Promised Land. Ten of them returned with a terrifying, exaggerated report about giants and unbreachable cities, causing the Israelites to panic.

"The people broke into loud cries, and the people wept that night."

— Numbers 14:1

According to the Talmud, that night was the 9th of Av. Seeing the people's absolute lack of faith, God declared: "You wept without cause; therefore, I will set aside this day as a day of weeping for all generations." That night sealed the fate of the Exodus generation to die in the wilderness — and it etched a tragic pattern into the very fabric of Jewish time.

A Timeline of Tears

History

The Tragedies of Tisha B'Av

הַצָּרוֹת שֶׁל תִּשְׁעָה בְּאָב

Astonishingly, across thousands of years of Jewish history, an uncanny number of national catastrophes have occurred on — or exactly around — this one specific date.

586 BCE

The Destruction of the First Temple

The armies of the Babylonian Empire, led by King Nebuchadnezzar, breached the walls of Jerusalem, slaughtered its inhabitants, and burned the magnificent First Temple (built by King Solomon) to the ground. The Jewish people were exiled to Babylon.

70 CE

The Destruction of the Second Temple

Following a long and brutal siege, the Roman Empire, led by General Titus, destroyed the Second Temple. Hundreds of thousands of Jews were murdered, and countless others were sold into slavery — sparking the longest exile in Jewish history.

135 CE

The Fall of Betar

The Romans crushed the Bar Kokhba revolt — the final Jewish rebellion for independence. The fortress city of Betar fell on Tisha B'Av, resulting in the massacre of over 500,000 Jewish men, women, and children.

136 CE

The Plowing of the Temple Mount

The Roman commander Turnus Rufus plowed the site of the Temple Mount and the surrounding area of Jerusalem, turning the holy city into a pagan Roman colony called Aelia Capitolina and forbidding Jews from entering.

1096 CE

The First Crusade

The First Crusade was declared by Pope Urban II in the season of Av. Crusaders swept through the Rhineland (France and Germany), wiping out entire Jewish communities and murdering tens of thousands of Jews.

1290 CE

The Expulsion from England

King Edward I signed the Edict of Expulsion, officially forcing all Jews to leave England. The deadline for their departure fell on Tisha B'Av.

1492 CE

The Spanish Inquisition and Expulsion

King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain issued the Alhambra Decree, ordering the expulsion of the entire Jewish population from Spain. The final deadline for every Jew to leave the country or convert was the 9th of Av, ending a glorious centuries-old era of Jewish life in the Iberian Peninsula.

1914 CE

World War I Begins

Germany declared war on Russia on Tisha B'Av, igniting the First World War. The devastation of WWI directly set the geopolitical stage for the rise of Nazi Germany and the Holocaust.

1942 CE

Deportations to Treblinka

The mass deportations of Jews from the Warsaw Ghetto to the Treblinka extermination camp began on the eve of Tisha B'Av.

How It Is Observed

Halachah

The Five Prohibitions

חֲמִשָּׁה עִינּוּיִים

Because the day represents such profound grief, it is observed with restrictions that mirror the strict mourning practices of Shiva — the first week of mourning after the death of a close relative. Tisha B'Av is a full 25-hour fast, and five specific activities are strictly forbidden.

1

No Eating or Drinking

A complete 25-hour fast — not even water. (Those whose health would be endangered are strictly forbidden from fasting.)

2

No Bathing or Washing

Washing for pleasure is prohibited, so that no physical comfort interrupts the experience of mourning.

3

No Creams or Cosmetics

Applying lotions, perfumes, oils, or cosmetics is forbidden for the duration of the fast.

4

No Leather Shoes

Historically a sign of luxury and comfort. Canvas or rubber shoes are worn instead as a sign of humility.

5

No Marital Relations

Abstaining from physical intimacy to maintain focus on collective grief and spiritual reflection.

In the Synagogue

A Stark and Somber Atmosphere

בְּבֵית הַכְּנֶסֶת

The visual and emotional atmosphere on Tisha B'Av is unlike any other day of the Jewish year.

Sitting Low

Just like mourners, people sit on the floor or on low stools rather than on regular chairs — a physical expression of being brought low by grief.

Dimmed Lights

Synagogues are dimly lit, sometimes illuminated only by candlelight, reflecting the darkness of the day itself.

The Curtain is Removed

The Parochet — the decorative curtain covering the Holy Ark where the Torah scrolls are kept — is removed or drawn aside, symbolizing the vulnerability and desecration of the Temple.

No Torah Study: Because the study of Torah is considered the greatest joy in a Jew's life ("The precepts of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart"), regular Torah study is forbidden on Tisha B'Av. One is only allowed to study sad texts — such as Lamentations, the Book of Job, or the Talmudic accounts of the Temple's destruction.
The Liturgy

Sacred Readings

Eicha and the Kinot

אֵיכָה וְקִינוֹת

Reading Eicha — The Book of Lamentations

On the night of Tisha B'Av, the congregation sits on the floor and mournfully chants Megillat Eicha (The Book of Lamentations). Written by the prophet Jeremiah, it is a heartbreaking, haunting eyewitness account of the destruction of Jerusalem and the suffering of its people.

"How does the city sit solitary, that was full of people! She has become as a widow…"

— Lamentations 1:1

Kinot — The Elegies

Throughout the day, the congregation recites Kinot — long, poetic dirges and laments written over centuries by great rabbis to mourn the Temples, the martyrs of the Crusades, the victims of the Inquisition, and, in modern times, the six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust.

Layers of Grief

Each Kinah was composed in a different era, for a different wound — and yet they are chanted together, one after another, as if all the catastrophes of Jewish history are a single, continuous mourning.

Hope After the Darkness

Meaning

The Hope of Redemption

תִּקְוַת הַגְּאֻלָּה

Despite the overwhelming darkness of Tisha B'Av, it is not a day of total despair. In Jewish tradition, mourning the past is the prerequisite for building the future. The Talmud states:

"Whoever mourns for Jerusalem will merit to share in her joy."

— Talmud, Ta'anit 30b

There is a profound, ancient Midrash which states that the Messiah will be born on Tisha B'Av. This teaches a powerful psychological and spiritual truth: redemption is born out of the very depths of tragedy. By remembering the brokenness of the world, the Jewish people actively long for its repair — Tikkun.

The fast concludes with the setting of the sun, and the mood subtly shifts from bitter weeping to quiet, resilient hope — a belief that one day, the tears of the 9th of Av will be transformed into the greatest joy, and Jerusalem will be rebuilt in peace.
